# coding interview practice: How to Practice Coding Interviews Without Wasting Three Months on Random Problems

Most people searching for coding interview practice are not actually short on problems. LeetCode alone has thousands of them, and free editorials, YouTube walkthroughs and Discord servers are everywhere. The shortage is structure. You know you should practice, you open a problem, you stall for twenty minutes, you read the solution, you feel like you learned something, and two days later you can't reproduce it.

That loop is the real problem, and it's worth diagnosing before you spend money on anything.

## What "practice" actually means in a coding interview

There's a difference between the three skills an interview tests, and most prep routines only train one of them.

- **Recognizing the pattern.** Given a problem statement you've never seen, can you tell within a couple of minutes whether this is two pointers, a sliding window, a heap, or DP? Interviewers spend a lot of the round watching this part.
- **Implementing it cleanly.** Once you know the approach, can you write bug-free code and handle the edge cases? This is the part grinding trains.
- **Explaining it out loud.** Half the round is verbal. You talk through your approach before you write anything, and you handle follow-up questions that change the constraints.

Sites like LeetCode are excellent gyms for the second skill. They're weaker on the first, because the problem you open is already tagged with the topic — the platform hands you the answer to the pattern-identification question. And no amount of solo grinding trains the third, which is why mock interviews exist as a separate category of tool.

The practical fix is splitting practice into stages instead of repeating one activity at high volume. Learn the pattern from a structured source, apply it to a handful of problems with a timer running, then drill pattern recognition separately by looking at many problems without writing full solutions.

## Why random grinding burns out

A common story in prep threads: someone solves 200 problems over three months, then freezes in a real interview on a problem they've technically seen before. The failure is usually retrieval, not knowledge. They memorized solutions instead of internalizing a template they can rebuild from scratch.

Two things help more than volume:

**Templates over solutions.** If you can write the sliding window skeleton from memory — the pointer movement, the window-state update, the shrink condition — you can reconstruct any specific variant in the round. If you only remember the answer to "Longest Substring Without Repeating Characters," you're stuck the moment the problem is reworded.

**Timed retrieval, not comfortable reading.** Reading a solution and nodding is passive. Being handed an unfamiliar problem and having to name the technique in 60 seconds is active. The second one is what the interview actually is.

There's a concrete illustration of the second point in one prep platform's design: AlgoMonster's Speedrun mode skips writing code entirely and gives you multiple-choice questions about which technique and template applies to each problem, so you can move through dozens of problems in a sitting instead of three. That's a recognition drill, not a coding drill — and it's a decent model even if you build your own version with a spreadsheet.

## A practice schedule that uses these tools properly

A workable eight-week plan, assuming an hour to ninety minutes a day:

**Weeks 1–4: pattern block.** One pattern per day. Read the explanation, study the template until you can write it from memory, then solve three to five mapped problems with a timer. Don't move to a new pattern until you can name the trigger conditions for the previous one without looking.

**Weeks 3–6: overlap with volume.** Keep adding patterns, and start running problems from a general bank on topics you've already covered. This is where you find out whether you actually retained the templates or just recognized them.

**Weekly: recognition drills.** Once a week, take twenty problems cold and spend one minute each naming the technique and the data structure involved. Don't solve them. Score yourself. This is the drill that most people skip and the one that pays off in the room.

**Weeks 6–8: mock interviews.** Two or three sessions minimum, with a person, where you talk through your approach out loud before coding. If you can't get a partner, record yourself and watch it back. It's uncomfortable and it works.

**Final week: review, don't learn.** Speedrun-style passes over every pattern, plus your own notes on the problems that tripped you up. New topics this late don't stick.
